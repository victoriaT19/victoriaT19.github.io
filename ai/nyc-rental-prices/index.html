NYC rental prices

An exploratory and statistical analysis of about 49,000 short-term rental listings in New York City, and a random-forest model that estimates a listing's nightly price.

Why I built it

This was my first end-to-end data science project, done as a data science challenge in early 2025. I wanted to practise the whole path on a real, messy dataset: cleaning it, exploring it, testing hypotheses with statistics, and only then fitting a model, so that the model would answer questions a person investing in a rental would actually ask.

What I built

  • Cleaning. Listings without a price or with a price of zero removed; prices above the 99th percentile treated as outliers (the maximum was $10,000 a night against a median of $106).
  • Exploration. Price by borough, room type and neighbourhood, a map of prices by latitude and longitude, and the words that appear most in the names of expensive and cheap listings.
  • Statistics. Pearson correlations, ANOVA with Tukey's test across room types, Welch's t-tests between pairs, a normality test and simple linear regressions.
  • Model. A scikit-learn pipeline with one-hot encoding for borough and room type and a 500-tree random forest on nine features: location, room type, minimum nights, reviews, host listings and availability.

Results

  • Location and room type drive the price. Manhattan averaged $197 a night against $88 in the Bronx; entire homes averaged $212 against $90 for private rooms.
  • Minimum nights and availability barely matter (correlations of 0.04 and 0.08), even if both are statistically significant with this many listings.
  • Names carry a signal. Expensive listings say "luxury", "loft", "village"; cheap ones say "room", "cozy", "private".
  • The model explains about half of the variance (R² 0.51, MAE $44.65, RMSE $73.08). Prices of the same kind of listing vary a lot, and the data has nothing about size, quality or photos.

What I learned

  1. Explore before modelling. The analysis answered most of the questions; the model mainly confirmed what the plots already showed.
  2. Significant is not the same as important. With 49,000 rows, a correlation of 0.04 has a tiny p-value and almost no practical effect.
  3. Heavy tails need a decision. One $10,000 listing distorts every mean; how outliers are handled changes the story.

Looking back

What I would do differently today, after the projects that came later:

  • compare the model with a simple baseline, such as the median price for each borough and room type, to know how much the forest really adds;
  • split train and test before deciding the outlier cut, so the test set plays no part in it;
  • model the logarithm of the price, which suits a skewed target better than the raw dollars.
