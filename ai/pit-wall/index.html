Pit wall

An F1 strategy engineer built only from public data: a tyre model, a simulation of the whole race, and a chat where an LLM answers strategy questions by running the same calculations. It is updated after every session of the remaining 2026 races.

Why I built it

In December 2025 I made a finishing-order predictor for the Abu Dhabi GP. It got right what everyone gets right (whoever starts at the front usually finishes there) and said nothing about strategy, the part of a race I find most interesting. During the 2026 season I turned it into something else.

I wanted to learn three things: how to model a system with a lot of noise (tyres, traffic, safety cars) and test it without fooling myself; how to evaluate forecasts honestly, using only what was known before each race; and how to put an LLM on top of real calculations so that every number it says comes from a tool, not from its memory.

What I built

  • Data. 68 races from 2024 to 2026 and 61,553 clean laps from OpenF1 and FastF1, plus Pirelli's compound nominations. One command updates everything after each session.
  • Tyre model. Fuel effect removed with a fixed rate; wear measured within each stint, which cancels the pace difference between cars. A small ridge regression beat gradient boosting at predicting wear for a race not yet run.
  • Strategy and race simulation. Monte Carlo over thousands of races with common random numbers, then a lap-by-lap simulation of the whole field: traffic, overtaking, dirty air, safety cars and retirements. The undercut is not a rule in the code; it appears when new tyres gain more than the gap.
  • Finishing-order model. A Plackett-Luce ranking model (driver and team strengths, as in van Kesteren & Bergkamp, 2023) averaged with a gradient-boosting model, with win, podium and top-10 chances scored by the Brier score.
  • The race engineer. Gemma 4 31B with 10 tools (strategy plan with what-if inputs, whole-field simulation, results, standings, prediction log). It runs on a chain of free models that switches in about a second when one is rate-limited, has a time budget per answer, and shows in the chat which model is answering and each tool it calls.
  • Dashboard. Gradio with a custom canvas replay of a simulated race on the real track shape, smoothed from telemetry.

The order model beats its first version in 47 of 68 races (p = 0.002), but beats the grid in 41 of 68 (p = 0.11), so it is better on average without being significantly better, and the grid still picks the winner more often. The simulation picks pit timing no better than a simple rule: a quarter of real first stops happen under a safety car, which no pre-race plan can see. It is a tool to compare options, not a forecast of the stop lap.

What I learned

  1. A simple baseline is mandatory. Without the historical rule I would have believed the strategy simulator worked.
  2. Leakage is rarely obvious. Using the race's own track temperature looked innocent. Later, the order model's first ensemble score was inflated by a tie-break that followed the real finishing order; fixing it took the score from 0.719 to 0.705.
  3. Fit the model to the shape of the problem. A race result is a ranking. Modelling it as one (Plackett-Luce) helped more than tuning another regressor.
  4. Small models win with little data. A three-variable ridge regression beat gradient boosting at predicting tyre wear.
  5. Measure before fixing. I was sure the simulated field spread out too much and proposed a fix. Measuring first showed the opposite; the fix would have made it worse.
  6. In an agent, the tool is the reliable part, and its text is part of the prompt. When a tool returned only numbers, the model explained uncertainty with the wrong cause. Returning a short "how to read this" fixed it.
  7. Free APIs fail a lot. A single free-tier call once took almost three minutes. A chain of free models, a time budget and live progress in the chat turned "it hangs" into a working demo.
