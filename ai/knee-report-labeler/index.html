Knee report labeler

An LLM reads knee MRI reports and grades 12 findings; a calibration on the few expert-labelled studies turns the grades into soft labels that train an image model.

Why I built it

Every training study in the competition had a free-text radiology report, but only 58 of 4,407 had expert labels for the 12 findings. I wanted to learn whether an LLM could be the labeler, a form of weak supervision, and how to tell how much each of its labels can be trusted. It was also my first time working with medical images and running a full pipeline within a free Kaggle GPU's limits.

What I built

  • Grading. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct reads each report, in any language, and grades every finding from 0 to 3 (not mentioned, equivocal, mild, moderate or severe) as one short line of JSON.
  • Defensive parsing. JSON first and a regex fallback, with failures counted (65 of 4,407, 1.5%).
  • Calibration. For each finding, the probability that an expert would mark it given the LLM's grade, estimated on the 58 labelled studies with smoothing and forced monotonicity. This learns the annotators' conventions, such as whether "small effusion" counts, without hand-written rules.
  • Engineering for a free GPU. Batches sized by a token budget and halved on out-of-memory errors, a resumable cache after every batch, and a TF-IDF fallback with no LLM. 4,407 reports took 90 minutes on two T4s.
  • The image model. A 2.5D EfficientNetV2 per MRI series, a small transformer across series and one attention query per finding, trained on the soft labels.

Tears, which reports state explicitly, come out strongest; osteoarthritis, described in many different words, comes out weakest. The image model reached a mean AUC of 0.840 public and 0.828 private as a late submission (the top of the leaderboard was around 0.96, with a single model on one fold and no ensembling). On the expert labels it scored 0.84, higher than the LLM grades it learned from, so the soft labels carried enough signal to generalize beyond them.

What I learned

  1. An LLM can be a labeler, if you measure it per label. The per-finding table is the real result: it says which soft labels to trust and which not to.
  2. Calibration on a few gold labels is worth more than prompt tweaks. 58 expert labels were enough to map the LLM's grades onto the annotators' conventions.
  3. Plan for the GPU to die. Resumable caches and adaptive batches turned session limits and out-of-memory errors into minor delays.
  4. Finish the whole pipeline early. The labeler was ready during the competition but the image model was not, so the score came two weeks late. Next time, a weak end-to-end version first.
